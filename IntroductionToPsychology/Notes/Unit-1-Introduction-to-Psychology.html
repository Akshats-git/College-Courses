3 Aug Lecture 1

Defining psychology

  • Cognition — anything that has to do with things going on in the mind.
  • Greek word psyche — soul.
  • A hub science — heavily cited in other disciplines.
Psychology
The scientific study of behaviour and mental processes. Ciccarelli & Misra (2021); Atkinson & Hilgard (2019)
Morgan & King
The science of psychology. Morgan & King (2025)

Behaviour

  • Anything that a person or animal does that can be observed in some way.
  • Can be recorded and studied — we see and hear it.
  • Measure what a person does and hears; the record helps make inferences about feelings, attitudes, thoughts and other mental processes.

Mental processes

  • All the internal, covert (hidden) activity of our minds — such as thinking, feeling, remembering.
  • Behaviour is the avenue to study mental processes.
  • Experiment and observation are the core.

Scope — and what it is not

  • Studying behaviour is not limited to psychology — sociology, economics, anthropology do it too.
  • Personal experience and common sense are not exactly what psychology is about.
Why scientific?

Why psychology has to be scientific

  • Theories summarise many observations and predict what can be expected to happen in new situations.
  • Researchers must observe them — a theory is not enough on its own.
  • Whenever a human observes anything, there is always the possibility that the observer will only see what they expect to see.
  • We don't need biases — use a systematic approach to study psychology scientifically.

Findings intuition would not have given us

  • Brain damage and face recognition — prosopagnosia; now wider.
  • Fundamental attribution error — attributing traits to people rather than to the situation.
    • Contrast this with the over-justification effect: a moment where social context makes us do something — we can justify that it doesn't come from within, it is for the external thing.
  • Childhood amnesia — almost no one can recall events from the first 3 years of life.
  • Obesity — National Family Health Survey: significant changes in walking and talking; increased vulnerability to diabetes is high. Psychology is interested in the factors that lead people to eat too much.
  • Media violence and children's aggression — the claim that watching violence on TV causes children to be more aggressive has been controversial.
    • Although many observers believe televised violence affects children's behaviour, others suggest that watching violence has a cathartic effect.
    • One factor seems to be a history of deprivation.

Historical roots

  • Socrates, Plato and Aristotle posed fundamental questions about mental life:
    • What is consciousness?
    • Are people inherently rational or irrational?
    • Is there free choice?
  • Hippocrates — father of medicine.
The oldest argument

Nature–nurture debate

The question of whether human capabilities are inborn or acquired through experience.

  • Nature view — human beings enter the world with an inborn store of knowledge and understanding of reality. Descartes
  • Nurture view — knowledge is acquired through experience and interactions with the world.
  • John Locke — at birth the human mind is a tabula rasa, a blank slate on which experience 'writes' knowledge and understanding as the individual matures.
  • This perspective gave birth to associationist psychology. Associationists denied that there were inborn ideas or capabilities.
    • Instead they argued that the mind is filled with ideas that enter by way of the senses and then become associated through principles such as similarity and contrast.
  • Most psychologists today take a more integrated approach.
The lab opens

The beginning of scientific psychology

  • Wilhelm Wundt — the father of scientific psychology; established the first lab.
  • The big idea — mind and behaviour, like planets or chemicals or human organs, could be the subject of scientific analysis.
Introspection
Observing and recording the nature of one's own perceptions, thoughts and feelings.
  • E.g. people's reports of how heavy they perceive an object to be, and how bright a flash of light seems to be — placing a rock on your hand.
  • Supplemented by experiments.
  • Analogy — it's like being asked to describe, step by step, the exact taste, texture and smell of a new food you are trying.
Five ways of looking at the mind

Schools of thought

Structuralism

E. B. Titchener
  • Goal — to analyse the basic elements, or 'structures', of the mind.
  • Analogy for engineers — think of it as reverse-engineering the mind.

Functionalism

William James
  • Focused on how the mind allows people to function in the real world — how people work, play and adapt to their surroundings.
  • Influenced by Darwin.

Behaviorism

John B. Watson
  • Rule — for psychology to be a true science, it must focus only on what is public and observable behaviour.
  • The mind is a black box. We can't see inside, so we shouldn't guess. We can only study the stimulus (input) and the response (output).
  • Core belief — nearly all behaviour is a result of conditioning, where the environment shapes us by reinforcing certain habits.
  • B. F. Skinner — for example, giving children cookies to stop them from whining reinforces (rewards) the habit of whining.

Gestalt Psychology

Wertheimer · Koffka · Köhler
  • Appeared in Germany around 1912. It is a German word for 'form' or 'configuration'.
  • Key figures included Max Wertheimer and his colleagues, Koffka and Köhler.
  • Primary interest — perception (patterns and background).
  • Central idea — 'the whole is different from the sum of its parts'.
  • Our minds automatically organise stimuli into meaningful patterns; we perceive a unified whole, not just isolated bits of information.

Psychoanalysis

Sigmund Freud
  • The unconscious mind shapes behaviour.
  • Personality — id (instincts), ego (reality), superego (morals).
  • Core concept — the unconscious: the part of our mind holding thoughts, wishes and impulses that we are unaware of. Early experiences influence adult life.
Conscious levelEgo & Superego

Later developments

The cognitive revolution

The mind returns. After WWII it became clear that earlier theories like behaviorism were too restrictive.

  • Information-processing — the development of computers in the 1950s gave psychologists a powerful new tool to theorise about the mind as an information processor.
  • Psycholinguists — Noam Chomsky's work on language suggested that complex mental structures were required for speech, something simple conditioning couldn't explain.
  • Neuropsychology — discoveries revealed clear relationships between events in the brain and mental processes.
  • Cultural psychology.

Psychology as a science

Science
A body of systematised knowledge that is gathered by carefully observing and measuring events. Morgan & King (2025)
How do things work. Ciccarelli et al. (2023)
  • Systematic observation — psychologists observe and measure behaviour under controlled conditions.
  • Testable scientific theories — the organisation of observed facts. Psychological theories are falsifiable and refined based on findings.
  • Measurement — the assignment of numbers to objects or events according to certain rules.
    • Space — accurately identify the blinking of a light; gives insight into the area of perception.
    • Time — how many times in 5 minutes did you pick up the phone? Like this.
  • Empirical evidence — data is gathered through experiments, surveys and observation.
  • Replication — studies are designed to be repeated to verify results.
  • Peer review — research is critically evaluated by experts before publication.
Description → Explanation → Prediction → Control

The four goals of psychology

01

Description

Start by observing.

Observe a behaviour and note: what is happening? Where does it happen? To whom does it happen? Under what circumstances does it happen?

02

Explanation

Build a theory.

Why is this happening? A tentative explanation helps understand the observed behaviour.

03

Prediction

When will it happen again?

Theories summarise many observations and predict what can be expected in new situations.

04

Control

How can it be changed?

Control means modifying behaviour from undesirable to desirable.

Theory
A general explanation of a set of observations or facts. Description supplies the observations; explanation helps build the theory.

The applied side

The application of psychology

  • The applied side is about "solving real life problems".
  • The application of knowledge to practical problems is an "art" — a skill or knack of doing things.
  • E.g. a psychotherapist talking to a worried client; the educational psychologist suggesting a new curriculum to a school.
  • Experience is needed — you can't become one by reading a book.

Psychological professionals and areas of specialisation (in the US)

Psychologist Psychiatrist Psychiatric social worker
  • Has a doctorate degree (PhD, PsyD, EdD) and works with either humans or animals in a variety of settings, based on the area of specialisation.
  • Must be licensed to practise independently.
  • Typically does not prescribe medication, but can go through specialised training to do so in a few states.
  • A medical doctor who specialises in the diagnosis and treatment of psychological disorders.
  • Can prescribe medication.
  • Has training in the area of social work (M.S.W.).
  • Often has a professional license to practise (L.C.S.W.).
